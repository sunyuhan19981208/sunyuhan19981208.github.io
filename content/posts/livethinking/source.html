Abstract.

The real-time nature of e-commerce livestreaming requires digital avatars to respond immediately to viewer comments, ensuring conversational fluency and seamless user experience. This goes beyond typical chatbot functionality, requiring not only correctness and helpfulness but also ultra-low latency. This poses a significant challenge for state-of-the-art Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems powered by Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), which often incur high inference delays despite their strong reasoning capabilities. To mitigate this latency-quality trade-off, we propose LiveThinking, a two-stage optimization framework. First, we employ knowledge distillation via Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) to compress a large teacher LRM into a lightweight architecture, significantly reducing computational cost. While this drastically lowers inference cost, the distilled model inherits verbose reasoning paths that still exceed latency budgets. In the second stage, we apply reinforcement learning via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to directly shorten the reasoning path, with a reward function that prioritizes response brevity while preserving correctness and helpfulness. LiveThinking demonstrates strong performance on both public benchmarks and real-world deployments. On the multi-hop reasoning dataset MuSiQue, our 30B MoE model (3B active) outperforms a 670B teacher in EM (+12.3) and F1 (+10.2), with 41% shorter responses and 95% lower decoding cost. Deployed on Taobao Live, LiveThinking reduces computational cost by 30× (12× from distillation and 2.5× from reasoning path compression), while improving response correctness by 3.3% and helpfulness by 21.8%, and drives significant growth in both commercial outcomes (order conversion rate) and user engagement (conversation turns), demonstrating its effectiveness in high-traffic production environments.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Efficient Reasoning, Reinforcement Learning, Mixture-of-Experts, E-commerce Customer Service

1. INTRODUCTION

AI-powered livestreaming leverages conversational agents as real-time e-commerce assistants, forming a challenging Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) scenario (Lewis et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023). The system must dynamically retrieve relevant product information and generate factually accurate responses under strict latency constraints. Response correctness, helpfulness, and low latency are all critical, directly impacting user engagement and sales performance.

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), such as OpenAI-o1 (OpenAI, 2024), Gemini 2.5 (Comanici et al., 2025), and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025a), excel at complex reasoning and generating accurate, nuanced responses. These capabilities are essential for achieving correctness and user-centric helpfulness in AI-powered e-commerce livestreaming. However, their end-to-end response latency can range from tens to hundreds of seconds, due not only to their large-scale parameters but also to lengthy reasoning trajectories. Such delays are unacceptable in interactive settings like live streaming, where audience engagement critically depends on timely responses. Consequently, LRMs face significant challenges in deployment within real-time, low-latency applications.

To reduce the response latency of LRMs for deployment in real-time applications, we propose a practical two-stage optimization framework. The first stage focuses on transferring the reasoning capabilities of a large-scale teacher model to a lightweight student model. Specifically, we distill a 670B teacher model (DeepSeek-R1) into a 30B Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model Qwen3-30B-A3B(Yang et al., 2025), which only activates 3B parameters during inference. To ensure that the student learns high-quality reasoning paths, we employ Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) (Gou et al., 2021; Gupta and Agrawal, 2022), guided by an LLM-based judge that filters out incorrect or unhelpful generations. This yields a distilled model that closely approximates the teacher’s response quality while operating at significantly reduced computational cost. However, due to imitation learning, the student model reproduces the teacher’s lengthy reasoning paths, resulting in end-to-end latency that exceeds industrial requirements despite its compact size. To address this inefficiency, we introduce a second stage that explicitly shortens reasoning trajectories while preserving response quality. Specifically, we employ reinforcement learning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) to refine the student model’s reasoning process. The approach uses a multi-objective reward function that jointly optimizes for reasoning brevity, correctness, and helpfulness, encouraging the model to generate concise yet high-quality responses. As a result, it not only reduces inference latency but also enhances output quality.

We validate the effectiveness and generalization of our framework through comprehensive evaluations across industrial, public, and production settings. On the industrial Tblive-E-Commerce QA benchmark, our final model achieves a 30× reduction in computational cost while surpassing its 670B teacher by +3.3% in correctness and +21.8% in helpfulness. On the public multi-hop reasoning dataset MuSiQue, our model exceeds the teacher’s performance with +12.3 Exact Match and +10.2 F1-Score, generates 41% shorter responses, and reduces decoding cost by 95%. These results highlight the effectiveness of our framework in balancing response quality, reasoning efficiency, and latency, which are key requirements for deployable interactive systems. We deployed the framework in Taobao Live’s DigitalLive services in July 2025, replacing a 14B dense production model. It has since driven a 59.0% increase in order conversion rate, leading to improved GMV performance and a 50.5% rise in the proportion of multi-turn conversations, indicating higher user engagement.

Our contributions are as follows:

  • •

    A two-stage optimization framework for deploying LRMs in real-time settings. We propose a synergistic pipeline that combines knowledge distillation with reinforcement learning, addressing the challenge of balancing correctness, helpfulness, and low latency in industrial AI assistants.

  • •

    Identification and mitigation of inherited reasoning inefficiency in distilled models. We reveal that standard distillation leads to replication of verbose reasoning paths from teacher to student, and introduce a targeted reinforcement learning stage using GRPO to compress reasoning trajectories while preserving output quality explicitly.

  • •

    An efficient MoE-based reasoning model validated on large-scale e-commerce livestreaming. Our approach achieves significant computational efficiency gains and improved response quality over the teacher model, demonstrating the feasibility of high-performance, low-latency reasoning systems in interactive applications.

2. RELATED WORKS

2.1. Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) addresses critical alignment challenges in LLMs—including hallucinations, harmful content generation, and instruction-following failures—by optimizing models to reflect human preferences through reward modeling (Ouyang et al., 2022). Modern approaches leverage Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) to automate preference scoring using LLM judges (Lee et al., 2023), while optimization techniques like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) often focus on singular objectives such as helpfulness or harm reduction. Building on recent innovations, GRPO (Guo et al., 2025a) improves training efficiency and robustness by adopting relative advantage estimation within grouped candidate responses.

2.2. Efficient Reasoning

Enhancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs through RL is a prevailing trend (Guo et al., 2025b; OpenAI, 2025). However, this often induces the “overthinking” phenomenon, where models produce computationally expensive and verbose Chain-of-Thought (CoT) paths (Chen et al., 2024). A prominent line of research addresses this by integrating length-based rewards or penalties into the RL framework to encourage conciseness. This approach is heavily influenced by seminal works that analyze the trade-off between reasoning length and stability (Yeo et al., 2025), and implement RL-based pruning strategies, such as O1-Pruner (Luo et al., 2025) and the techniques behind Kimi k1.5 (Team et al., 2025). RAG-RL (Huang et al., 2025) uses GRPO to leverage the RAG reasoning capability of the LLM. These methods typically leverage RL to penalize verbosity, a strategy also adopted by numerous other studies to prune or shorten CoT chains while maintaining performance (Hou et al., 2025; Ning et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025).

3. PRELIMINARIES

3.1. Problem Definition

We define a conversational commerce assistance task where, given a viewer question about products, the system must generate helpful and accurate responses. Formally, for each interaction at turn t, the inputs consist of Retrieved Product Documents (Dt), which include relevant product specifications and attributes retrieved from the catalog; the Audience Question (Qt), representing the current natural language query; and the Conversation History (Ht={(Q1:t−1,R1:t−1)}), which provides the preceding dialogue context. Based on these inputs, the system outputs a Thought Process (Tt) detailing the step-by-step reasoning, and a Final Response (Rt) as the natural language answer to the viewer’s query.

3.2. Evaluation Framework

Responses are evaluated along two dimensions using LLM-as-a-Judge:

Correctness (C)

Measures factual alignment with product knowledge:

C⁡(Dt,Qt,Ht,Rt)={1if ​Rt​ accurately reflects ​Dt,0otherwise.

A response is deemed correct if and only if all of its claims are verifiable against the provided documents (Dt), it maintains logical consistency with the conversation history (Ht), and it serves as a direct and pertinent answer to the current query (Qt). This ensures the response is not only factually sound but also avoids being non-responsive or “off-topic."

Helpfulness (H)

While correctness is a fundamental requirement, optimizing for correctness alone can yield undesirable model behavior. For instance, a model might become overly cautious, generating minimal responses to minimize the risk of hallucination. Although factually correct, such terse answers deliver a poor user experience by failing to be genuinely assistive.

To counteract this tendency, we introduce Helpfulness as a complementary metric. It incentivizes the model to provide richer, more valuable information, striking a balance between accuracy and utility:

H⁡(Qt,Ht,Rt)={1if ​Rt​ proactively assists the user ​Qt,0otherwise.

A response is considered helpful if, in addition to correctly answering the query, it proactively offers supplementary, relevant information from the documents that anticipates and addresses the user’s implicit needs within the conversational context (Ht).

Validating the LLM Judge

To ensure the reliability of our LLM-as-a-Judge, we validated its performance against human judgment. We began by manually annotating a corpus of several hundred of responses for both Correctness and Helpfulness. These were treated as two independent binary classification tasks. Each response was labeled by two annotators, and to maintain high data quality, instances with conflicting labels were discarded. We then balanced the positive and negative samples for each task to a 1:1 ratio, creating two distinct benchmark datasets. Through iterative refinement of the judge prompt, our LLM judge achieved an accuracy of 83% on the Correctness benchmark and 84% on the Helpfulness benchmark, demonstrating strong alignment with human evaluation.

4. METHODOLOGY

Our two-stage methodology first distills e-commerce reasoning ability into a lightweight model via RFT. Subsequently, we employ GRPO to compress the model’s reasoning path, optimizing for efficiency while maintaining response quality.

4.1. E-Commerce RAG Reasoning Ability Distillation based on RFT

To transfer capabilities from the 670B reasoning MoE teacher model (37B activated parameters) to the lightweight student model, we employ a three-stage distillation pipeline formalized as:

Teacher-based Data Generation

Given an unlabeled e-commerce corpus 𝒰={(Dt(i),Qt(i),Ht(i))}i=1N, we use the teacher model Pθteacher with temperature-scaled decoding to sample k distinct candidate trajectories {(Tt(i,j),Rt(i,j))}j=1k for each instance i. This process results in a candidate set of trajectories, where each full data point is a tuple containing the original input and a generated output.

Rejection Sampling by LLM Judge Filtering

To ensure the quality of our distillation data, we introduce a filtering step that retains only the most valuable trajectories. A trajectory is considered valuable if it is evaluated as both correct and helpful. We employ an LLM-as-Judge, implemented via DeepSeek-R1, to perform this evaluation based on the metrics defined in §3.

Specifically, for each input instance i, we iterate through its k generated trajectories. If at least one trajectory (Tt(i,j),Rt(i,j)) satisfies both conditions, we select one such valid trajectory to be included in our final distillation dataset, denoted as 𝒟distill. If none of the k trajectories for a given input pass the filters, the instance is discarded entirely. The final dataset is constructed as follows:

𝒟distill⊆⋃i=1N⋃j=1k{τ(i,j)∣C⁡(τ(i,j))=1∧H⁡(τ(i,j))=1},

where τ(i,j)=(Dt(i),Qt(i),Ht(i),Tt(i,j),Rt(i,j)), and the judgment functions for correctness C⁡(⋅) and helpfulness H⁡(⋅) are applied accordingly. The subset notation (⊆) reflects our strategy of selecting at most one trajectory per original input instance.

Student MoE Model Fine-tuning

Since our student model θstudent is a Mixture of Experts (MoE) model, we train it by minimizing a composite loss function. This objective includes the primary sequence-to-sequence task loss and an auxiliary loss for load balancing. The total optimization objective is:

ℒtotal=ℒSFT+λaux⋅ℒaux.

The main task loss ℒSFT trains the model to reconstruct teacher outputs:

ℒSFT=−𝔼(D,Q,H,T,R)∼𝒟distill​[∑i=1Lylog⁡Pθstudent​(yi∣𝐱,y<i)],

where the input 𝐱=[D;Q;H] is the concatenated context, and the target sequence y=[T;R] encodes the thought T and response R. The term ℒaux represents the MoE auxiliary loss, which promotes balanced routing of tokens across experts. Its contribution is scaled by the hyperparameter λaux, ensures stable training and effective expert specialization.

4.2. Jointly Optimizing Reasoning Efficiency, Helpfulness, and Correctness with RL

While RFT (as detailed in §4.1) transfers knowledge, it also inherits the teacher’s verbose reasoning. We address this using RL to optimize for reasoning efficiency, correctness, and helpfulness.

RL Problem Formulation

We frame response generation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The state st is the concatenated input context [Dt;Qt;Ht], and the action at is a token sequence [Tt;Rt] generated autoregressively by the policy πθ, a student model with parameters θ. An episode consists of the complete generation of an action at and terminates upon producing an end-of-sequence (EOS) token.

Reward Design

To enhance the model’s reasoning efficiency while ensuring the quality of its responses, we propose a multi-objective RL optimization framework. This framework is implemented through a set of meticulously designed reward functions. First, we introduce a length-based reward function to optimize for computational efficiency. Concurrently, we formulate correctness and helpfulness reward functions to constrain the model, thereby guaranteeing that the pursuit of efficiency does not compromise the quality of its outputs.

Length Reward (rlength): Optimizes reasoning efficiency relative to reference model outputs:

Lupper =λupper​Lref,Llower=λlower​Lref,
d ={max⁡(Llower−Lpolicy,0)Lpolicy<Llower,max⁡(Lpolicy−Lupper,0)Lpolicy>Lupper,0otherwise,
rlength =max⁡(0,1−dϵ).

The length reward rlength, is calculated based on how the length of a policy-generated sequence Lpolicy, compares to an acceptable length range. This range is determined by the length of a reference sequence OPENLref). The reference sequences are generated from the reference model, which in our case is the student model after the RFT stage. Specifically, we define an upper bound Lupper=λupper​Lref and a lower bound Llower=λlower​Lref. The coefficients λupper and λlower control the desired compression ratio and prevent over-compression, respectively. The deviation d measures how far Lpolicy is outside the acceptable interval [Llower,Lupper]. Finally, the reward rlength is computed by penalizing this deviation, with a hyperparameter ϵ serving as a tolerance margin that dictates how sharply the reward decreases with the deviation.

Correctness Reward (rcorrect): Reduces hallucinations by verifying response fidelity to product documents:

rcorrect=C⁡(Dt,Qt,Ht,Rt)∈{0,1},

where 1 indicates Rt is fully consistent with Dt and answers Qt without fabrication.

Helpfulness Reward (rhelpful): Prevents over-conservatism by assessing practical utility:

rhelpful=H⁡(Qt,Ht,Rt)∈{0,1},

where 1 denotes Rt provides actionable shopping guidance addressing implicit needs in Ht.

Group Relative Advantage Calculation

To derive a normalized signal for policy optimization, we first compute a composite reward rj for each of the K responses in a batch (Eq. (1)). This score is a weighted sum of its correctness, helpfulness, and length-based rewards. The advantage score Aj is then calculated by normalizing rj using the mean μ and standard deviation σ of the rewards across the batch, as shown in Eq. (2). This normalization transforms the raw rewards into standardized Z-scores, providing a stable signal for policy updates. A small constant ϵstd (e.g., 10−8) ensures numerical stability.

(1) rj =wc​rjcorrect+wh​rjhelpful+wl​rjlength,
(2) Aj =rj−μσ+ϵstd,where ​μ,σ=mean, stddev​({ri}i=1K).

Intuitively, this mechanism rewards responses that perform better than average within a mini-batch, making training more stable and less sensitive to absolute reward scales.

The GRPO objective maximizes advantage-regularized returns:

where η=0.2 is the clip range, β=0.01 controls KL penalty, and sj=(Dt,Qt,Ht), aj=(Tt,Rt). We choose GRPO over standard PPO due to its improved training efficiency and stability, particularly in scenarios with complex, multi-objective rewards.

5. EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS

5.1. Datasets

We evaluate our approach on two distinct datasets, each containing 8,000 training and 400 test samples.

Tblive-E-Commerce QA

This proprietary dataset is sourced from live shopping streams on our e-commerce platform. Each sample comprises a conversation history, a viewer’s real-time question, and relevant structured product information that was dynamically retrieved based on user intent. This dataset encapsulates key industrial challenges, such as reasoning over noisy conversational context and grounding responses in structured merchandise data.

MuSiQue

As a public benchmark for multi-hop question answering, MuSiQue tests a model’s complex reasoning ability. Each question is designed to be unanswerable from any single document, requiring the model to synthesize information across multiple paragraphs. We utilize the distractor setting, where each question is paired with approximately 10 documents, most of which are irrelevant distractors. This setup rigorously evaluates the model’s capacity to identify and connect relevant facts from noisy, knowledge-intensive contexts, complementing our industrial dataset.

Evaluation

Evaluation protocols differ across the test sets: On Tblive-E-Commerce QA, responses are assessed using the LLM-as-a-Judge framework from 3.2 for both correctness (C) and helpfulness (H). For MuSiQue, we follow standard evaluation practice using Exact Match (EM) and F1 scores to measure factual accuracy.

5.2. Experimental Setup

We implement our two-stage training framework using specialized infrastructure for each phase. The distillation phase employs our internal Magatron-based framework for RFT, processing the filtered 𝒟filtered dataset through large-scale distributed training. For the RL phase, we utilize the ROLL framework (Team and Contributors, 2025) to implement the GRPO described in §4.2. Reward configurations are adapted per dataset: For Tblive-E-Commerce QA, we apply the composite reward with components defined in 4.2 (correctness, helpfulness, and length rewards). For MuSiQue, we maintain the length reward but substitute correctness assessment with EM as a binary reward (rcorrect=EM score), while omitting helpfulness due to the dataset’s non-conversational nature. 6. EXPERIMENTS

6.1. Main Results

Table 3 presents the performance of our proposed framework on both the industrial Tblive-E-Commerce QA and public MuSiQue benchmarks. Our final model, LiveThinking (Qwen3-30B-A3B + RFT + RL), achieves state-of-the-art results across all metrics while operating at industrial-scale efficiency.

On the Tblive-E-Commerce QA dataset, LiveThinking attains 92.3% correctness and 96.8% helpfulness, surpassing its 670B teacher (DeepSeek-R1: 89.0%, 75.0%) and all baselines by significant margins. Despite being 22× smaller in total parameters and activating only 3B parameters during inference, it reduces decoding computation to just 0.92 TFLOPs, which is over 30× less than the teacher, while generating responses that are more than 2.7× shorter. This demonstrates that careful optimization enables small models to exceed larger teachers in both quality and efficiency. More case studies are detailed in Appendix C.

Consistent gains are observed on MuSiQue: LiveThinking achieves 53.5 EM and 67.2 F1, outperforming the 670B teacher (41.2 EM, 57.0 F1) and setting a new state-of-the-art among models with comparable or even much higher computational cost. Response length is reduced from 807 to 473 tokens, confirming the generalizability of our approach beyond domain-specific data.

Notably, our framework also achieves strong performance on a dense 8B Llama backbone: the DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-Llama-8B + RFT + RL variant improves correctness from 20.8% to 86.3% and helpfulness from 34.5% to 93.8%, while reducing TPR from 332 to 152. This demonstrates the generality of our two-stage optimization approach across different model architectures.

6.2. Ablation Study

To evaluate the role of each stage, we conduct an ablation study on the Qwen3-30B-A3B backbone (Table 3). As expected, distillation with RFT improves correctness from 67.3% to 81.3% and helpfulness from 56.5% to 77.0%, confirming its ability to transfer high-quality reasoning behaviors. However, the student inherits the teacher’s verbose reasoning patterns, resulting in only a slight reduction in response length, i.e., 396 vs. 353 TPR. RL alone achieves higher correctness (87.5%) and helpfulness (90.0%) with significantly shorter responses (TPR: 152), demonstrating that policy optimization can simultaneously improve both quality and efficiency. Crucially, when RFT is used to initialize the model before RL, the final performance further increases to 92.3% correctness and 96.8% helpfulness. This significant gain suggests that the RFT stage provides a stronger behavioral prior by aligning the model with correct multi-step reasoning, which the RL stage can then refine more effectively under latency constraints. Thus, the two stages are complementary: RFT establishes a solid foundation of reasoning capability, while RL compresses and optimizes it for deployment. The synergy enables performance beyond what either method can achieve independently.

6.3. Efficacy of Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning

To validate the effectiveness of RFT in the first stage of our framework, we compare it with standard SFT, where the student learns from the same teacher model but without data filtering. As shown in Table 4, RFT outperforms SFT on both correctness and helpfulness, with a substantial gain in the latter (+5.0%). We attribute this to the nature of the two metrics under open-ended e-commerce QA. Correctness is well-grounded in provided product information, allowing SFT to preserve it effectively through imitation. In contrast, helpfulness varies significantly across reasoning paths and depends on nuanced aspects such as clarity, structure, and user intent alignment—qualities that are not uniformly present in the teacher’s outputs. By filtering trajectories using an LLM judge, RFT ensures the student learns only from high-quality, well-articulated responses. These results demonstrate that careful curation of training data—rather than indiscriminate imitation—is essential for transferring complex, user-centric reasoning patterns in real-world applications.

6.4. Impact of Target Reasoning Length

A key aspect of our RL-based compression stage is balancing reasoning brevity and response quality. To investigate this trade-off, we train variants of the model with different target reasoning lengths, controlled by adjusting the coefficients λu​p​p​e​r and λl​o​w​e​r in the reward function. Figure 4 presents the results of this sensitivity analysis. The results reveal a clear trend: when the brevity constraint is too strong, the model generates excessively short reasoning paths that lack sufficient intermediate steps, leading to drops in correctness and helpfulness. As the constraint is relaxed, performance improves, peaking at an intermediate setting where the model maintains enough internal structure to reason effectively while remaining concise. However, when the brevity constraint is too weak, the model generates unnecessarily long reasoning paths that often repeat key premises or introduce irrelevant thoughts, leading to performance degradation. Our goal is to identify an optimal operating point that significantly reduces the average number of tokens per response—thereby lowering latency—without compromising correctness and helpfulness scores. This experiment validates the specific brevity target used for our final model.

6.5. Ablation Study of the Multi-Objective Reward Function

We conduct an ablation study to demonstrate the indispensability of each component in our multi-objective reward function: correctness (Rcorrect), helpfulness (Rhelpful), and brevity (Rlength). As shown in Table 5, removing any single component leads to a notable degradation in the corresponding metric.

Notably, including the brevity reward not only reduces latency but also enhances both correctness and helpfulness. This suggests that optimizing for brevity encourages the model to discover more efficient and accurate reasoning paths. Furthermore, when helpfulness is not optimized (e.g., Rcorrect+Rlength), the model becomes overly conservative to reduce hallucinations, sacrificing helpfulness. In contrast, simultaneously optimizing for helpfulness and correctness forces the model to genuinely improve its RAG reasoning capabilities to mitigate hallucinations, rather than merely avoiding them, resulting in a significant boost in correctness (91.5 vs. 87.3). These findings confirm that all three components are essential for holistically addressing the correctness-helpfulness-latency trilemma.

6.6. Architectural Analysis: Efficacy of MoE

To justify our choice of a MoE architecture, we compare its perform-ance-to-cost ratio against traditional dense models. We benchmark our final model, built on Qwen3-30B-A3B (30B total, 3B active), against a range of dense models spanning from 4B to 32B parameters. Figure 5 illustrates that the MoE architecture achieves performance approaching that of the large dense model (about 7.5B) in our scenario while requiring a fraction of the computational cost during inference. It also vastly outperforms the small dense model. This confirms that MoE is the most efficient architectural choice for our latency-critical application.

7. CONCLUSION

In this paper, we introduce LiveThinking, a two-stage training framework that adapts LRMs to real-time applications by balancing quality and latency. Our approach first leverages RFT to distill reasoning ability from a 670B teacher into a lightweight 30B student model. Recognizing that standard distillation inherits the teacher’s verbose reasoning trajectories, we introduce a second stage that applies GRPO with a multi-objective reward function to explicitly compress reasoning paths while further enhancing correctness and helpfulness. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that LiveThinking achieves a remarkable 30-fold reduction in computational cost, while simultaneously outperforming its much larger teacher on correctness and helpfulness. Deployed at scale on Taobao Live, LiveThinking demonstrates the feasibility of deploying low-latency, high-performance reasoning models in interactive e-commerce applications. This work provides an effective paradigm for deploying powerful yet efficient conversational AI in real-world, time-sensitive settings.

8. ETHICAL USE OF DATA

This research adheres to ACM’s policies on research involving human subjects. Our proprietary Tblive-E-Commerce QA dataset was fully anonymized by removing all Personally Identifiable Information (PII) to protect user privacy; the MuSiQue dataset is a public benchmark. Ethical risks such as misinformation are directly mitigated by our methodology: the Correctness reward minimizes hallucinations, while the Helpfulness reward promotes genuine user value. The research was conducted in accordance with our organization’s internal data governance and ethics review policies.
